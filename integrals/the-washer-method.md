## Plane regions separated from the axis of revolution

The washer method is used to determine the volume of a solid of revolution that has a cavity inside it. Before introducing the procedure and the formulas, however, a preliminary remark is needed.

By this point, the geometric purpose of integrals should be clear. The types of integrals considered so far may also have proved less daunting than the opening of this section suggested. This relative simplicity will not last, however, as later topics require more demanding techniques.

When we introduced [definite integrals](../definite-integrals/), we saw that they measure the signed area between a curve $\gamma$ and the $x$-axis: regions above the axis contribute positively, while regions below it contribute negatively.

We then saw that integrals can also be used to compute volumes, particularly those of solids formed by rotating a plane region $R$ about the $x$-axis. The [disc method](../the-disc-method/) computes such a volume using the following definite integral:

$$V = \int_a^b \pi \big(f(x)\big)^2 \ dx$$

Rotating the region about the $x$-axis produces a solid with no central cavity, so its volume is relatively straightforward to compute.


![IMG. 1](svg/the-disk-method-1.svg)

The figure shows the case of a single curve generated by the function $f(x)$, which determines a region $R,$ lying entirely above the $x$-axis.

How does the situation change if a second curve $g(x)$ lies between the $x$-axis and $f(x)?$ Suppose that $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a,b]$ and satisfy $0 \lt g(x) \lt f(x)$ throughout the interval.

![IMG. 2](svg/the-washer-method-1.svg)


In this case too we can identify, between the two curves, a plane region $R$ similar to the one in the first figure, but with an immediate difference: the region $R$ is separated from the $x$-axis by the condition $g(x) > 0$, and therefore rotating it about that same axis generates a hollow solid, whose outer profile is determined by the function $f(x)$ and whose inner profile is determined by $g(x).$


![IMG. 3](svg/the-washer-method-2.svg)


At each $x_0,$ a plane perpendicular to the $x$-axis cuts $S$ in an annulus with outer radius $f(x_0)$ and inner radius $g(x_0).$

The area of an annulus is the difference between the area of the disc of radius $f(x)$ and that of the disc of radius $g(x)$, and is therefore equal to:

$$A(x) = \pi(f(x))^2 - \pi(g(x))^2 = \pi\left[(f(x))^2 - (g(x))^2\right]$$

![IMG. 4](svg/the-washer-method-3.svg)

Keep in mind that the difference is taken between the squares of the two radii, and not between the radii, because we are subtracting areas.

## Determining the volume

We now come to the heart of the volume computation. First, we divide the interval $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = (b-a)/n,$ each determined by the points:

$$a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b$$

We can denote the generic subinterval by $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ and we choose a point $x_k^{*}$ inside it, at which to measure the two radii.

For small $\Delta x,$ the portion of $S$ over $[x_{k-1},x_k]$ is approximated by a washer of thickness $\Delta x,$ outer radius $f(x_k^{*}),$ and inner radius $g(x_k^{*}).$ Its approximate volume is the difference between the volumes of the corresponding cylinders:

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta V_k &= \pi(f(x_k^{*}))^2\Delta x - \pi(g(x_k^{*}))^2\Delta x \\[6pt]
           &= \pi\left[(f(x_k^{*}))^2 - (g(x_k^{*}))^2\right]\Delta x
\end{align}
$$

Summing the contributions of all the portions obtained from the individual subintervals gives an approximation of the volume of $S$:

$$V \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} \pi\left[(f(x_k^{*}))^2 - (g(x_k^{*}))^2\right]\Delta x$$

The right-hand side is a [Riemann sum](../riemann-integrability-criteria/) of the function $\pi(f^2 - g^2)$ and since $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a,b],$ their difference $f^2 - g^2$ is continuous as well, so the integrand is Riemann integrable and the sum converges to the [definite integral](../definite-integrals/) as $\Delta x \to 0.$

As $n$ increases the individual portions become thinner and approximate the profile of the solid better. In this case the volume of $S$ is:

$$V = \pi\int_a^b \left[f^2(x) - g^2(x)\right] \ dx$$


By the linearity of the integral we can also write:

$$V = \pi \left[\int_a^b f^2(x) \ dx - \int_a^b g^2(x) \ dx\right]$$

The first term gives the volume of the outer solid, while the second gives the volume removed to form the cavity.

When $g$ is zero the annulus degenerates into a full disc and the formula reduces to that of the disc method, which is a limiting case of the washer method.

- - -

The washer method does not require the axis of revolution to be a coordinate axis. Since the formula depends only on the two radii, both can be measured from the chosen axis. Plainly, if the rotation takes place about a horizontal line $y = c$ with $c \leq g(x) \leq f(x),$ the outer radius of the annulus becomes $f(x) - c$ and the inner one $g(x) - c.$ The volume can therefore be written as:

$$V = \pi\int_a^b \left[(f(x)-c)^2 - (g(x)-c)^2\right] \ dx$$

When instead the axis lies above $f$ and $g$, that is $g(x) \leq f(x) \leq c,$ the distances are measured in the opposite direction and the two radii become $c - g(x)$ and $c - f(x).$ In this case it is as if $f$ and $g$ were interchanged, so that $g$ gives the outer radius and $f$ the inner one. The volume must therefore be rewritten as in the following formula:

$$V = \pi\int_a^b \left[(c-g(x))^2 - (c-f(x))^2\right] \ dx$$

When the rotation takes place about the $y$-axis, the roles of the two variables are exchanged and the region must be described in the form $0 \leq q(y) \leq x \leq p(y)$ for $y \in [a,b].$ In this case the volume is determined by:

$$V = \pi\int_a^b \left[(p(y))^2 - (q(y))^2\right] \ dy$$

For a generic vertical line $x = c,$ the radii must instead be measured from that line. If $c \leq q(y) \leq p(y),$ the volume is:

$$V = \pi\int_a^b \left[(p(y)-c)^2 - (q(y)-c)^2\right] \ dy$$

If instead $q(y) \leq p(y) \leq c,$ the outer radius is $c-q(y)$ and the inner one is $c-p(y),$ so the volume is:

$$V = \pi\int_a^b \left[(c-q(y))^2 - (c-p(y))^2\right] \ dy$$

The same principle applies to rotation about the $x$-axis. Care is still needed when identifying the outer and inner radii, since reversing them gives an incorrect result.

## Example 1

We now work through a few typical examples, computing the volume of the solid generated by rotating about the $x$-axis the region between the line $y = x$ and the [parabola](../parabola/) $y = x^2.$

The two curves meet when $x = x^2,$ that is at $x = 0$ and $x = 1,$ and on the interval $[0,1]$ we have $x^2 \leq x.$ Thus $f(x)=x$ gives the outer radius, while $g(x)=x^2$ gives the inner radius.

![Img. 3](svg/the-washer-method-4.svg)

Using the formula for the annulus we obtain

$$V = \pi\int_0^1 \left[x^2 - (x^2)^2\right] \ dx = \pi\int_0^1 \left(x^2 - x^4\right) \ dx$$

By linearity, we integrate the two terms separately:

$$
\begin{align}
V &= \pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1 \\[6pt]
  &= \pi\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5}\right) \\[6pt]
  &= \pi \cdot \frac{5-3}{15} \\[6pt]
  &= \frac{2\pi}{15}
\end{align}
$$

The solid generated by rotating the region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ about the $x$-axis therefore has volume exactly $2\pi/15.$

## Example 2

We now consider the same region as in the previous example, but we rotate it about the line $y = -1.$ This rotation obviously produces a solid different from the one obtained in Example 1, with a cavity that now runs through its whole length. Note that the two curves lie above the line $y = -1,$ so the distances are measured upwards. The outer radius is therefore given by the distance between the axis and the farther curve, that is the line $y = x$:

$$f(x) - c = x - (-1) = x+1$$

Applying the same procedure to the parabola gives the inner radius:

$$g(x) - c = x^2 - (-1) = x^2+1$$

The volume is computed by substituting the two radii into the formula:

$$V = \pi\int_0^1 \left[(x+1)^2 - (x^2+1)^2\right] \ dx$$

Expanding the squares gives, without difficulty, a fourth-degree [polynomial](../polynomials/) inside the integral sign:

$$
\begin{align}
(x+1)^2 - (x^2+1)^2 &= x^2 + 2x + 1 - x^4 - 2x^2 - 1 \\[6pt]
                    &= -x^4 - x^2 + 2x
\end{align}
$$

Since the integrand is a polynomial, we can integrate it term by term:

$$
\begin{align}
V &= \pi\int_0^1 \left(-x^4 - x^2 + 2x\right) \ dx \\[6pt]
  &= \pi\left[-\frac{x^5}{5} - \frac{x^3}{3} + x^2\right]_0^1 \\[6pt]
  &= \pi\left(-\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{3} + 1\right) \\[6pt]
  &= \pi \cdot \frac{-3-5+15}{15} \\[6pt]
  &= \frac{7\pi}{15}
\end{align}
$$

The volume $7\pi/15$ is greater than the volume $2\pi/15$ found in Example 1, even though the same region is rotated in both cases. Moving the region farther from the axis makes each point trace a circle of larger radius and therefore produces a larger volume.


## Example 3

As a final example, consider a sphere of radius $R$ with a cylindrical hole of radius $a$ drilled through its center along a diameter, where $0<a<R.$ We compute the volume of the remaining ring.

Our sphere is the solid generated by rotating a semicircle $y = \sqrt{R^2-x^2},$ while the hole is generated by rotating the horizontal line $y = a.$ As in the previous cases, the remaining solid is therefore generated by the region between the two curves, with outer radius $f(x) = \sqrt{R^2-x^2}$ and inner radius $g(x) = a.$

The limits of integration are the abscissas at which the two curves meet, so in our case $x = \pm\sqrt{R^2-a^2}$. We set $c = \sqrt{R^2-a^2},$ and obtain:

$$V = \pi\int_{-c}^{c} \left[(R^2-x^2) - a^2\right] \ dx$$

Since $R^2 - a^2 = c^2,$ we can rewrite everything in terms of $c$:

$$V = \pi\int_{-c}^{c} \left(c^2 - x^2\right) \ dx$$

By a fortunate coincidence the function inside the integral sign is even (symmetric with respect to the $y$-axis), so the integral over the interval $[-c,c]$ is exactly twice the integral over $[0,c]$ and is easily computed as:

$$
\begin{align}
V &= 2\pi\int_{0}^{c} \left(c^2 - x^2\right) \ dx \\[6pt]
  &= 2\pi\left[c^2x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^{c} \\[6pt]
  &= 2\pi\left(c^3 - \frac{c^3}{3}\right) \\[6pt]
  &= \frac{4\pi c^3}{3}
\end{align}
$$

Substituting the original value for $c$, the volume obtained is:

$$V = \frac{4\pi}{3}\left(R^2-a^2\right)^{3/2}$$

The result can also be rewritten in terms of the height $h$ of the remaining ring, that is the distance between the two circles bounding the openings of the hole. Since $h = 2c,$ substituting $c = h/2$ gives:

$$V = \frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{\pi h^3}{6}$$

As can be seen, the volume of the ring depends only on its height, and not on the radius of the original sphere, so two rings of the same height have the same volume even if they are obtained from spheres of very different size.

## Some final considerations

So far we have assumed that $g(x) \leq f(x)$ throughout $[a,b].$ If the curves cross at $x=c,$ split the interval at $c$ and use the appropriate outer and inner radii on each subinterval, as in [finding areas](../finding-areas-by-integration/).

The appropriate method depends mainly on the position of the axis relative to the region and on how the region is described. If the axis forms part of the boundary, the perpendicular cross sections are discs, so the [disc method](../the-disc-method/) applies.

When a region described in terms of $x$ is rotated about a vertical axis, or a region described in terms of $y$ is rotated about a horizontal axis, the washer method may require rewriting the boundary curves in terms of the other variable.

If the cross sections perpendicular to the axis are not annuli, the washer formula does not apply. In that case, use the general cross-section method based on the area $A(x)$ of each slice.
